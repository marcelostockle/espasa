Written at Cambridge and printed in London, the Principia opens with definitions and the three laws of motion, then runs through three books.

- **Book 1** treats motion without resistance: orbits along conic sections and Kepler's second law, derived from a central force.
- **Book 2** treats motion through resisting media, including fluids and pendulums, and argues against Descartes's vortex theory.
- **Book 3**, on the system of the world, applies the inverse-square law of gravitation to the Moon's irregularities, comets, the tides and the precession of the equinoxes.

The book grew out of Edmond Halley's 1684 visit and Newton's tract *De motu corporum in gyrum*. The Royal Society had spent its book budget on another title, so Halley paid for the printing himself. In 1686, when Book 1 was presented, Robert Hooke claimed that Newton had taken the inverse-square idea from him; Newton denied it and nearly withheld Book 3, but Halley persuaded him to go on. In the end he acknowledged Wren, Hooke and Halley in a scholium.

Its mathematics was accepted readily, while gravity as action at a distance troubled Huygens and Leibniz. Newton revised the work twice: the second edition (1713, edited largely by Roger Cotes) added the General Scholium, and the third appeared in 1726. The first English translation, by Andrew Motte, came out in 1729.
